Where I disagree "Act on your behalf" is true. With it, Nulo signs this app's authorization requests without asking whenever the call is on the app's list (packages/wallet-bridge/src/dispatcher.ts, handleCreateAuthWit); off-list calls still prompt. What the card leaves out is the limit: those signatures only take effect inside a transaction you approve. The worse problem is one nobody flagged: "Transaction simulation" says "Nothing is sent to the network", yet it lets the app read your private balances without asking, and it's rated lower than the two permissions that always ask.

What each permission really does

  • Send transactions (▲ HIGH): every send opens an approval window no matter what (dapp-interaction/service.ts). The permission is the app's list of allowed calls: anything off the list is refused without a popup, and the list also limits the signing above. That's why it exists.
  • Transaction simulation (● MED): simulations run on your device, but the results go back to the app, and they include decrypted private values (view-executor.ts). The tools app uses this to read your private token balances (apps/tools/src/lib/capabilities.ts).
  • Contract registration (— LOW): public metadata only. Private data, which the tools app doesn't ask for, hands over your whole address book and decrypted events.
  • Ratings are fixed per permission type, so every app gets two ▲ HIGH on its first connect. You can only disconnect the whole app later, not take back one permission.

What the research says

  • Warnings you see every time stop being read: brain scans show habituation from the second exposure (Anderson et al., CHI 2015). In one study, 17% of Android users looked at permission screens and 3% understood them (Felt et al., SOUPS 2012).
  • Popular wallets don't grade connect requests. MetaMask says "See your accounts and suggest transactions"; Rabby rates risk per transaction, when it simulates it. Phantom splits what an app can see from what it can do.
  • ERC-7715 still wants wallets to "warn them against granting dangerous permissions" (EIP). So flags stay, for the dangerous cases only.
  • GitHub groups app consent by what the app can do, and "Act on your behalf" is its own heading there (GitHub docs).

What changes

  • Two groups replace the cards: what the app can do on its own, and what always asks. The first answers the question a new user actually has ("what happens if I click Connect?"); the second is the reassurance.
  • No HIGH/MED/LOW. A normal request shows zero warnings, so a warning means something. The orange tag is kept only for: a permission Nulo doesn't recognize (still off until ticked), one you denied before, an "any contract" scope, and address-book access.
  • Sentences come from facts Nulo can check: a token name only when the contract is a token Nulo knows. Anything else falls back to "Read private data from 2 contracts" plus the list in Details.
  • The per-permission switches move into Details. They still work exactly as today.

Clicks

Connect grants what's listed. Details expands in place. Rename for this app opens the name field from item 2.

Effort: medium. New copy, a scope-to-sentence mapper with tests, new layout. Before shipping, confirm the "only count inside a transaction you approve" line against the account contract. I'm fairly confident it holds for Nulo's accounts: a private signature can only be checked by this wallet, and public ones need a transaction.

What changes

  • Nulo grants only the account at connect, then pauses the app's first call that needs more and asks, one thing at a time.
  • Apps written against the SDK expect an answer to what they asked for. The tools app retries after a refusal; others may just break.

Effort: large. A new kind of prompt, partial grants, and a compatibility risk with every dApp. The most reassuring first screen, and the most windows overall.

My pick: A, with B's copy shipped first

A is the only option that answers "what happens if I click Connect?" at a glance and is still honest about the one thing that happens without asking: private reads. It drops the ratings that taught your tester to ignore the screen, and it keeps the switches for people who want them. B's strings are the cheap first PR and carry over into A's Details. C shows the calmest first screen, but it puts more windows in front of people and breaks the request–answer contract dApps rely on.

Confidence: high that the ratings should go and the simulation line must change; moderate on A's exact layout.
