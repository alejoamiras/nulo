The one-sentence diagnosis

The codebase's conventions are healthy — what recurs instead is a single failure shape: a shared primitive exists, but only its raw mechanics are exposed, so every call site hand-rolls the convenient wrapper and the copies drift. The lock guard (68 hand-rolled sites), the promise-memoize idiom (6 copies, newest added four days before the audit), the client exhaustiveness guard (16 files), and the clipboard helper (~19 sites, one already-fixed bug reintroduced seven times) are all the same story at different layers.

Verified findings — architectural

architecturalverified · highQ-01

Lock has no withLock() — the acquire/release protocol is hand-rolled at 68 call sites

What it is. The wallet's services all guard their writes with a shared mutual-exclusion lock. The lock only offers raw "enter" and "leave" operations, so all 14 service files repeat the same three-line ritual around every write — 68 times. Three files have already invented their own local variations of it.

What gets harder. The safety rule ("always release, even on error") lives in developer discipline, not in the code. One new write method that forgets the release step silently freezes that service for every later caller — the exact failure the lock's emergency timer exists to paper over.

The fix. Give the lock a single withLock(fn) method that does the ritual once, correctly, and convert the 68 sites to it. Mostly mechanical; a handful of sites with extra error-handling around the critical section need individual care.

useFeeEstimation and useFeeEstimationMap are two hand-maintained copies of one state machine

The fix. The map version is the general case — rebuild the single-estimate composable as a thin wrapper over it with a fixed key, deleting ~90 duplicated lines. Verified feasible without changing what components see; two genuine behavioral seams need preserving deliberately.

structuralverified · highQ-05

The client exhaustiveness-guard skeleton is copy-pasted in 16 of 23 service clients

What it is. Sixteen service-client files repeat an identical five-part boilerplate whose only purpose is a compile-time proof that no RPC method was forgotten. The shared factory documents that callers should add this guard — but its signature can't enforce it, so everyone pastes it.

What gets harder. A new client (or a lazy edit) that skips the pasted guard silently loses the proof. Nothing fails; the safety just evaporates.

The fix. A curried generic whose type constraint is the guard — verified by actually compiling it under the repo's strict TypeScript: both failure modes (missing method, typo) still error at compile time. Each client shrinks to a method list and one call.

Cross-cutting

House rule worth adopting: when a shared helper is used at three-plus sites through the same ritual, the ritual belongs in the helper. Five of the fifteen findings (Q-01, Q-04, Q-05, Q-06, Q-09) are this one rule violated at different layers.

Security surfaces attract duplication risk disproportionately: the secret-export scrub (Q-06), the identity strip (Q-08), and the dispatcher authorization (Q-13) are all trust-sensitive paths where copy drift is a security event.
